# Mr. Sixpounder: Scene 1 - The Fourteenth Landing

The Tuple rose from the bald patch on the fourteenth landing at 2:47 AM, and I watched it arrive the way one watches a hymn one has already memorized settle into the congregation's mouth. The Weave beneath the carpet had been exposed for eleven days. I had filed the replacement request myself, and the request had been misrouted, and the misrouting had been acknowledged, and the acknowledgment had been filed, and so here we were. The geometry of the raw Weave pulsed faintly beneath the threadbare nothing where carpet had been, and from that pulse: a shape. Shoulders first. Then a tie. Then a face I recognized, though the Weave had negotiated away most of the details worth recognizing.

Dettmer. Or rather, what remained of Dettmer after the copying process had finished with him. The name badge read DTTMR. The tie was knotted in a configuration that does not exist in any knot taxonomy maintained by this hotel or any other, and I have consulted them all. The eyes were open and fixed on a point approximately four feet behind the wall, staring with what I can only describe as vague administrative purpose. It did not move. It would not move. They never do.

I adjusted my cuffs. The gesture is load-bearing. It holds the sentence together the way the seams of this suit hold its shape, which is to say: through repetition, through ritual, through the quiet insistence that things which have been maintained will continue to hold. The suit is charcoal. The seams have become their own color. I have been here a long time.

But the Tuple could wait. The Tuple would wait whether I wished it to or not, because waiting is the only sacrament the Weave-Born observe. Kovac, however, had not checked in for forty minutes, and Kovac was somewhere in the Eastmark Stairwells, and Mr. Sixpounder's patrol was due to pass through in thirteen minutes, and I am not in the business of losing new hires to ignorance when procedure exists precisely to prevent it.

I left the Tuple standing in its small circle of exposed grammar and climbed.

The stairwells of the Eastmark rise in tight, identical spirals, lit by fluorescent tubes that hum at a frequency I stopped hearing years ago the way one stops hearing one's own heartbeat. The carpet is ancient. It has outlived the demons who installed it and will outlive the demons who walk it now. In places, it has worn so thin that the Weave shows through like scripture visible beneath a palimpsest, and I have learned to step around those places the way the faithful step around altars, not from fear but from respect for what might rise.

I found Kovac on the sixteenth landing. She was flattened against the wall with both hands pressed to the concrete behind her as though she could push through it by faith alone, and her eyes were the eyes of someone who has just learned that the world contains categories she was not briefed on.

"Kovac."

She made a sound. It was not a word.

"Kovac, look at me. Not at the stairs. At me."

Her eyes found mine and I saw in them the old terror, the first terror, the one that every new hire carries through the door and that the hotel files away in its own time. I know this terror. I have carried it myself, long ago, before I understood that the things which walk these halls are not aberrations but residents, and that residency is a covenant older than fear.

"There is a man," she said. "There is a man on the stairs and he is made of -- his fingers are --"

"Mr. Sixpounder," I said. "Permanent Resident, subsection Non-Responsive. He patrols the Eastmark Stairwells nightly from 2:40 to 4:15 AM and has done so for decades. His schedule is in the binder at the East 14 station. You would have found it between the vending inventory and the incident log, had you looked."

"His *fingers* --"

"Three are original. The remainder have been replaced by cutlery over time. A salad fork. A butter knife. Something that may have been a corkscrew. Maintenance has catalogued them." I adjusted my cuffs again. "The protocol is simple. You stand still. You speak evenly, if you must speak at all. And you never, under any circumstance, offer him anything from the minibar."

"Why?"

"Because the last person who did is now a decorative installation on the ninth landing, and I do not have the forms to process another one this quarter."

She stared at me as though I had spoken in a language she once knew and had since forgotten, and I felt for her the way one feels for all newcomers to a faith they did not choose: with tenderness, and with the understanding that tenderness will not spare them what comes next.

What came next was the sound. *Hitch. Drop. Hitch.* The rhythm of a body that has forgotten how to walk but remembers that walking is required, and so performs the act through sheer contractual obligation. It came from the landing above us, and Kovac's breath stopped, and I placed one hand on her shoulder and said, "Be still."

He rounded the corner.

The chestplate had fused to the ribcage beneath it into a corroded hybrid of iron and bone that maintenance had long since categorized as structural, because what else do you call a thing that is neither armor nor skeleton but refuses to be separated into either? Carpet fibers had woven into his shins over the decades, grey-brown threads trailing from the greaves like roots from something planted too long in one place. The sword hung at his side, and the cutlery fingers clicked against the pommel in a rhythm that was not quite deliberate and not quite involuntary: *tick, tick, tick*, the sound of a clock that measures something other than time. He smelled of towel detergent -- he is laundered on a dedicated housekeeping cycle -- with an underlying copper note that I have never been able to fully attribute.

He passed within four feet of Kovac. She did not breathe. He did not acknowledge her. He turned his head, briefly, toward the wall beside me, the way he always does, the way he has done every night for longer than I have served this hotel, and I have served this hotel for a very long time. Then he continued down the stairs. *Hitch. Drop. Hitch.* The sound diminishing like a hymn receding into the nave.

"Come," I said. "We are going back down."

We descended together, Kovac's hand on the railing in a white-knuckled grip that I did not comment on because commentary would not have served her. The fourteenth landing appeared below us, and the Tuple was still there, still staring at its fixed point of vague administrative purpose, still wearing a tie knotted in a configuration that should not exist.

"What is that," Kovac said. It was not a question.

"A Tuple. Weave-Born. It rose from the exposed patch approximately twenty minutes ago. It has copied Dettmer from Accounts, though the resemblance is, as you can see, approximate." I reached for the clipboard and pen that had been clipped to the railing for exactly this purpose, because this hotel prepares for its crises the way temples prepare for feast days: with the certainty that they will come, and with the forms already printed. "It will need to be filed."

I placed the clipboard in her hands. She looked at it. She looked at me.

"Welcome to Hotel Stackz, Kovac. The forms will make sense before the building does. I suggest you start with the forms."
