I propped my axe butt on the brick and stood ready. "Listen up. That weld seam holds ten-odd breaths at most; outside, even heaven-punish, earth-destroy cannot cut through. Go out is a dead road. We go down."

That bunch of dog-faces grew restless as crickets.

"Go down." Herry, after a good while, squeezed out only two words: "Go down is eighteen layers of hell."

"Down is floor. Our feet already press one hundred thirty-three layers."

"Your skull cracked, right?" said Phansie Le, eyes wide as moonmilk. "Every stone squats on a worm, every cubic meter overflows. You still mean to drag us all to hard death?"

A young mulcher's hand went loose; his pulse rifle, clang-dang, fell ground-ward. "Die here, die there, not all one virtue."

"Pick it up!" Ulakhan's roar came like a whip across the face. "Eight thousand years of heritage, and you let it smash broke?" The subordinates stared, waiting on a word; the boss pinched his chin and made no sound.

"Since peace came then," I said, taking up the thread, "those things climbed the stairs up one hundred thirty-four floors. The stairs are still there, no one watching. Their main force now runs wild in our head barracks. So: we keep to the ground, pass through one layer of hell, feel for them at the stairs behind, and go up. Along Saint Smalin Avenue, then up one hundred thirty-five, and bring back four hundred seal soldiers."

"Then who enters hell first?"

"Who saw me last?" I let those words hang. "I walk front, for this road is the only one. Now, who follows?"

Ulakhan stared at me a good while, hunting a flaw on my face; I gave him the face of a boiled potato. He nodded once.

"Boss," said Joiyuss, eyes brightening, "if our way happens past the supply station, our car still holds two hundred credit points on deposit."
